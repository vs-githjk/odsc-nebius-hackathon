# Fact-check: “A ChatGPT query uses ten times the energy of a Google search.”

Historically traceable, but not a reliable universal figure

The claim traces primarily to a February 22, 2023 Reuters interview with Alphabet chairman John Hennessy. He estimated that an interaction with a large language model would cost roughly ten times as much as a conventional keyword search.

However, Hennessy was discussing financial cost, not a direct measurement of electricity consumption.

In October 2023, researcher Alex de Vries used that comparison alongside Google's 2009 estimate of search electricity consumption. The result was approximately 3 watt-hours per AI query versus 0.3 watt-hours per Google search.

Goldman Sachs, the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI), and subsequent news reporting widely circulated that comparison in 2024.

The critical finding is that an estimate about cost evolved into an apparently precise claim about electricity consumption.&#x20;

## 2. Where the claim changed

2009 — Google

Numerical baseline

A conventional search consumes an estimated 0.3 Wh of electricity, according to Google's internal calculation.

February 2023 — John Hennessy / Reuters

A large-language-model interaction is estimated to cost ten times more financially than a keyword search.

The 10× comparison enters the documented record.

October 2023 — Alex de Vries

Crucial shift

The financial-cost comparison is used to infer an electricity figure of approximately 3 Wh per LLM interaction.

The inference is cross-checked against SemiAnalysis's modeled operating costs, rather than validated through direct ChatGPT electricity measurements.

2024 — IEA, Goldman Sachs and EPRI

The estimated 2.9-to-0.3 Wh comparison becomes a headline-ready numerical statement that ChatGPT uses nearly ten times more electricity.

2025 onward — Reassessment

Improved hardware and different assumptions about query length produce much smaller estimates for common text interactions.

The original comparison is no longer a dependable description of an average current query.

The particularly important wording change is “costs 10 times more” to “uses 10 times more electricity.” Those quantities are related, but they are not interchangeable: inference costs also reflect hardware prices, utilization, depreciation and other expenses.

The October 2023 article is the clearest identified publication making that conversion into the approximately 3 Wh figure.&#x20;

## 3. How many independent sources actually support it?

# 2

# 0

Verified direct 10× measurements

The two core numerical source families are Google's 2009 internal estimate for traditional search and the early-2023 SemiAnalysis modeling of ChatGPT's inference infrastructure. Hennessy's estimate was a separate expert judgment about cost, not a measured energy figure.

De Vries combined those foundations into a comparison. The IEA, Goldman Sachs and EPRI subsequently amplified the estimate; they did not independently measure both services in equivalent conditions.

So there are two foundational numerical source families, but zero verified independent, head-to-head electricity measurements establishing the precise tenfold ratio. This does not mean there is no independent research into AI electricity consumption; it means that research does not directly validate this particular universal claim.

In February 2025, Epoch AI estimated that a typical GPT-4o query could consume around 0.3 Wh, roughly one-tenth of the widely circulated older figure. Its authors identified major assumptions behind the earlier estimate, including older A100 hardware and unusually long responses.

On June 10, 2025, Sam Altman gave a figure of approximately 0.34 Wh for an average ChatGPT query. He did not publish enough methodological detail alongside the statement for independent reproduction.&#x20;

## 5. Final verdict

Earliest underlying numerical source: January 11, 2009 — Google's estimate of 0.3 Wh per search.

Earliest verified explicit tenfold comparison: February 22, 2023 — John Hennessy's remarks reported by Reuters, referring to financial cost.

Key conversion into an electricity claim: October 18, 2023 — Alex de Vries's analysis in Joule.

Major popularization: January–May 2024 — the IEA, Goldman Sachs and EPRI.

Independent support for the exact ratio: No verified direct head-to-head measurement. The familiar 10× figure was assembled from older numerical estimates and assumptions rather than measured as a universal fact.

A defensible replacement would be:

> Early estimates suggested that a ChatGPT interaction could require roughly ten times the electricity of a traditional Google search. However, the figure was based on modeling and older data, and newer estimates indicate substantially lower energy consumption for many ordinary AI text queries.

That preserves the historical finding without presenting a dated estimate as an established fact about all current ChatGPT requests.
