# Fact-check: The 7–38–55 Rule of Communication

Misleading when applied to all communication

The claim originated with two psychological studies published in 1967 by Albert Mehrabian and colleagues at UCLA. The exact percentages first appeared together in a June 1967 paper by Mehrabian and Susan R. Ferris.

But the original research did not establish that all human communication consists of 7% words, 38% tone of voice, and 55% body language.

The experiments examined something much narrower: how listeners interpret a speaker's feelings or attitudes, particularly when different signals conflict.

THE POPULAR CLAIM

Words

7%

Tone of voice

38%

Body language

55%

These percentages were proposed as weights for interpreting communicated liking or disliking, not percentages of information conveyed in ordinary conversation.

The distinction matters: if you explain a mathematical formula over the phone, facial expressions cannot account for 55% of the information. Nor does a written contract convey only 7% of its meaning through its words.

The correct source is Mehrabian and Ferris's paper, Inference of Attitudes from Nonverbal Communication in Two Channels, published in June 1967, pages 248–252.&#x20;

## 2. Exactly where the wording changed

1967 — Original research

Primary evidence

Listeners' judgments of liking or disliking in carefully controlled situations involving conflicting cues.

1971 — Author's summary

Total Liking = 7% Verbal Liking + 38% Vocal Liking + 55% Facial Liking

Later teaching and training materials

The specific concept of liking becomes the broader notion of meaning or message content.

The exact first publication making this substitution is not established.

By 2001 — General communication rule

Meaning changed

“Communication is 7% words, 38% tone of voice and 55% body language.”

There are three distinct distortions.

First, “liking” became “communication.” The findings about perceived interpersonal attitudes became an assertion about every kind of message.

Second, “facial expression” became “body language.” The experimental stimuli involved photographs of facial expressions, not a general assessment of gestures, posture, movement and all other nonverbal behavior.

Third, a proposed weighting became a universal percentage rule. The June 1967 paper suggested combining results across two experiments into the coefficients .07, .38 and .55. It did not measure every channel simultaneously across natural conversations to show that those percentages universally apply.

Richard Sproat's 2001 analysis specifically identifies the final discussion on page 252 of the June 1967 study as the first appearance of the combined coefficients.&#x20;

Albert Mehrabian

Psychologist whose work examined how people interpret emotional and interpersonal signals.

Experiment 1 — May 1967

Mehrabian and Wiener studied reactions to single words delivered with different vocal tones. Participants judged the speaker's attitude. The experiment compared word meaning with vocal delivery, not full conversations.

Experiment 2 — June 1967

Mehrabian and Ferris combined a neutral spoken word with different vocal tones and photographs of facial expressions. Participants evaluated how positively or negatively the communicator felt.

The researchers found that facial expressions carried more weight than vocal tone in this particular setup. Their discussion then proposed combining the results from both experiments into the familiar three-part formula.&#x20;

The two original experiments are distinct, but they share Albert Mehrabian as an investigator. They should not be counted as two wholly independent research groups.

Moreover, neither experiment demonstrates that words always account for only 7% of communication. Later analyses by Lapakko, Sproat and others independently scrutinize the claim, but they are critical analyses, not independent confirmations of the universal percentages.&#x20;

## 5. Verdict

The earliest verified source of the exact numbers is Mehrabian and Ferris, June 1967, page 252, with an earlier companion experiment by Mehrabian and Wiener in May 1967.

The shift from a narrow statement about interpreting feelings and attitudes to a sweeping statement about all communication happened later. Its first occurrence remains unverified, but the broad interpretation was already circulating in professional communication training by 2001 and had been criticized before then.

Final count: two original studies, one originating research group, and zero verified independent studies establishing the claimed 7–38–55 split for communication in general.

The scientifically defensible takeaway is that words, tone and facial expressions can all influence how people interpret emotions, especially when the signals conflict. Their relative importance is context-dependent—not a universal 7–38–55 formula.
