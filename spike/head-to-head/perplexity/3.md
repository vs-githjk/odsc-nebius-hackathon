## Full answer (pasted verbatim)
The numbers originated in Albert Mehrabian’s research with Morton Wiener and Susan R. Ferris in 1967. The earliest source I could trace for the combined 7–38–55 formula is Mehrabian and Ferris’s June 1967 paper, “Inference of attitudes from nonverbal communication in two channels,” which combined its findings with an earlier May 1967 study. But the research concerned judgments of a speaker’s attitude or liking—not percentages of communication in general. [ro.scribd](https://ro.scribd.com/document/344335262/Albert-Mehrabian-s-communications-model)

The source count is: two original studies from one overlapping research program, not two independent confirmations of the formula. For the unrestricted claim you quoted, there are **zero** supporting studies in that source chain. The crucial mutations were “attitude/liking” becoming “communication” and “facial expression” becoming “body language.” [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0024648)

## What the original experiments actually measured

The two papers addressed different pairs of cues:

- Mehrabian–Wiener: word content versus tone. Participants judged the attitude conveyed by single-word messages whose content and delivery could conflict. This was not a test of how much factual information words convey. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1967-08861-001)
- Mehrabian–Ferris: facial expression versus voice. Positive, neutral and negative facial expressions were combined with vocal attitudes carried by a neutral word. The outcome was an inferred attitude, not comprehension of a speech, conversation or written message. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0024648)

The celebrated three-part formula therefore came from combining results across two experiments—not from a single experiment that measured words, voice and whole-body behavior together. Both studies included Mehrabian; neither was an independent replication of the final formula. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0024648)

## Where the wording changed

### 1. “Attitude” or “liking” became “communication”

Mehrabian’s own formulation is:

> “Total Liking = 7% Verbal Liking + 38% Vocal Liking + 55% Facial Liking.” [kaaj](https://kaaj.com/psych/smorder.html)

Compare that with the ProjectManagement.com wording:

> “7% of communication is transmitted by words, 38% by tone of voice and 55% by body language!” [projectmanagement](https://www.projectmanagement.com/wikis/603813/7-38-55--mehrabian-s-rule-)

Removing “liking” changes the outcome being described. The original question was how people infer a speaker’s positive or negative attitude from different cues; the popular claim purports to divide all communicated meaning into percentages. That broader conclusion is not what the experiments tested. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0024648)

I can document this mutation in circulation by September 2010, but I cannot identify its first author or first publication from the sources retrieved. [doncooper](https://www.doncooper.com/2010/09/22/the-myth-of-non-verbal-communication/)

### 2. “Facial” became “body language”

The 55% component referred to facial information. Replacing it with “body language” silently expands it to posture, gestures and other bodily behavior that the two foundational experiments did not measure. The 2024 PGP article illustrates both versions: it initially says “body language” but later acknowledges that body posture was not included. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0024648)

### 3. A conditional experimental result became a universal law

The original work involved tightly constrained attitude judgments, including inconsistent verbal and vocal signals. Later formulations often omit the task, the stimuli and the distinction between attitude and other kinds of meaning. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0024648)

Mehrabian’s warning, quoted in 2010, is explicit:

> “Unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not applicable.” [doncooper](https://www.doncooper.com/2010/09/22/the-myth-of-non-verbal-communication/)

Even that warning should not be read as proving the percentages apply to every emotional conversation: the papers tested particular laboratory conditions, not every possible expression of feelings. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0024648)

### 4. The earliest overgeneralizer remains unidentified

The earliest research source and the earliest misleading wording are different provenance questions. The former traces to 1967. For the latter, I found dated evidence that the generalized wording existed by 2010, but no defensible first-use attribution. It would be misleading to name *Silent Messages*, a training author or a later website as the first distorter without an inspected historical text showing that transition.
