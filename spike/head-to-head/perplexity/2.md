## Full answer (pasted verbatim)
The specific “100-word email = a bottle of water” claim originated in a September 18, 2024 Washington Post analysis, based on calculations supplied by UC Riverside researcher Shaolei Ren. It estimated 519 milliliters for GPT-4 generating a 100-word email at an average American data center—not a measured, universal cost of an AI-written email. [washingtonpost](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/09/18/energy-ai-use-electricity-water-data-centers/)

There is **one** identifiable originating analysis behind that specific figure, with no independent replication established in the chain I found. Its methodology traces further back to an April 6, 2023 research paper, but that paper made a different claim: a 500-milliliter bottle for roughly 20–50 GPT-3 questions and answers, not one email. [washingtonpost](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/09/18/energy-ai-use-electricity-water-data-centers/)

## Where the wording—and meaning—changed

### 1. The original bottle covered a conversation, not an email

The April 2023 paper’s exact formulation was:

> “ChatGPT needs to ‘drink’ a 500ml bottle of water for a simple conversation of roughly 20–50 questions and answers, depending on when and where ChatGPT is deployed.” [sciencealert](https://www.sciencealert.com/ai-chugs-a-bottle-of-water-every-time-you-chat-with-it)

That estimate concerned GPT-3. The paper’s footnote specifies assumed energy use, data-center efficiency, electricity-generation water intensity and a range of cooling-water intensities. It was a modeled estimate, not a water-meter reading for an actual chat. [sciencealert](https://www.sciencealert.com/ai-chugs-a-bottle-of-water-every-time-you-chat-with-it)

### 2. The Post introduced a separate GPT-4 email calculation

In September 2024, the task became one 100-word email and the model became GPT-4. The Post credited a new calculation by Ren using the earlier paper’s methodology. This is not simply an original “20–50” denominator accidentally disappearing: the Post’s email result was a separate estimate for a different model and task. [washingtonpost](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/09/18/energy-ai-use-electricity-water-data-centers/)

Ren later explained that the 2024 calculation used the energy estimate available from EcoLogits in mid-2024 and companies’ disclosed water-use-effectiveness information. That later explanation helps identify an upstream input, but it is not an independent confirmation of the resulting 519 milliliters. [deseret](https://www.deseret.com/u-s-world/2026/06/21/what-environmental-costs-do-data-centers-have/)

### 3. The qualifications disappeared in summaries

The Post’s full text specifies GPT-4, acknowledges large geographic variation and identifies an average American data center in its methodology. Its headline already compresses that into “A bottle of water per email.” illuminem then generalizes the task to “a 100-word response”; the LinkedIn wording “every time” goes further by making the estimate sound invariant. [washingtonpost](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/09/18/energy-ai-use-electricity-water-data-centers/)

Your version makes the final generalization: “written by AI” replaces a particular GPT-4 scenario, while “uses a whole bottle” no longer signals an estimate or its conditions.

### 4. Total water footprint became easy to mistake for cooling alone

The research methodology includes two components:

- Water consumed at the data center, principally through cooling.
- Water consumed off site to generate its electricity. [sciencealert](https://www.sciencealert.com/ai-chugs-a-bottle-of-water-every-time-you-chat-with-it)

Consequently, “a bottle of water” does not mean a server necessarily evaporated that entire amount at its own cooling tower while writing the email. Later reporting on Ren’s calculation explicitly distinguishes these components and says most of the original estimate was attributed to off-site electricity generation. [techradar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/data-centers-water-myths-and-what-the-numbers-really-say)

## What the originating researcher says now

In an August 10, 2026 article, Sentient quoted Ren directly:

> “The 2024 estimate for GPT-4 was time-specific, assumption-based, and should not be used to describe general AI/ChatGPT or today’s optimized systems.” [deseret](https://www.deseret.com/u-s-world/2026/06/21/what-environmental-costs-do-data-centers-have/)

That is the key distinction from the attention-span example: this claim has an identifiable researcher, an explicit methodology and a real originating calculation. The problem is that a conditional, historical estimate became a universal statement—not that the bottle-per-email number had no identifiable source at all. [washingtonpost](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2024/09/18/energy-ai-use-electricity-water-data-centers/)
