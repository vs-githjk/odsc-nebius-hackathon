## Full answer (pasted verbatim)
This claim comes from a real randomized clinical trial published online on October 19, 2026—not from an older study repeatedly recycled as new. Sharon K. Hunter and colleagues reported a 7.78-point difference in preschool IQ scores between children whose mothers received phosphatidylcholine during pregnancy and children whose mothers received placebo. The University of Colorado Anschutz publicized it the next day as “about eight points” at age four. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

There is **one** independent study behind this specific result. The university release, news stories, foundation article and social-media posts are reports of the same trial, not separate confirmations. Unlike several previous claims you asked about, the numerical core is traceable and substantially faithful to the original—but the short version omits important qualifications. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

## Where the wording changed

### 1. “7.78” became “about eight”—a reasonable rounding

The paper reports:

> “Increased WPPSI-IV Full-Scale IQ (mean difference 7.78, 95% CI 1.88, 13.7; p = .010).” [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

The university release says “about eight”; KTLA says “nearly eight”; the X post and your wording say eight. Rounding the point estimate is not itself misleading. Omitting the confidence interval, however, hides how imprecise the estimate is: the study’s interval extends from approximately two to fourteen points. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

### 2. “At ages three and four” became “at age four”

The university release and foundation article say cognitive tests were administered at ages three and four. Yet the university release’s author quote and key points summarize the finding as “at age 4,” and downstream articles repeat that framing. [news.cuanschutz](https://news.cuanschutz.edu/news-stories/prenatal-supplement-prenatal-supplement-linked-to-improved-child-cognition-and-social-behavior-study-says)

That age-four wording therefore arose at the institutional-publicity stage, not solely on social media. Without inspecting the full paper’s age-specific analysis, I would not assert that 7.78 is an estimate calculated exclusively from four-year-old assessments. The abstract calls it a result “through 4 years of age.” [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

### 3. A particular preparation and regimen became generic “choline supplements”

The trial randomized women to 9,000 milligrams daily of phosphatidylcholine or placebo, beginning at 16 weeks’ gestation and continuing until delivery. Phosphatidylcholine is a choline-containing compound; 9,000 milligrams of it is not 9,000 milligrams of choline. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

The compressed claim omits the preparation, dose and treatment period. That makes it sound as though the same result has been established for choline supplements generally, which this one regimen cannot demonstrate.

### 4. A group difference became an “IQ jump”

The *New York Post* used “8-Point IQ jump” in image text. The study actually compares average scores between randomized groups; it does not show individual children increasing eight points from a previous IQ baseline. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

Your wording—“scored … higher … than those in a placebo group”—preserves that distinction better than “IQ jump.”

### 5. The abbreviated claim drops the sample and outcome hierarchy

The study enrolled 206 pregnant women, but the IQ comparison involved 103 children: 54 in the phosphatidylcholine group and 49 in the placebo group. Behavioral follow-up included 142 children. These are different denominators, not contradictory enrollment counts. [ktla](https://ktla.com/news/taking-this-nutrient-during-pregnancy-may-boost-kids-iq-study-finds/)

IQ was a secondary outcome. The two primary outcomes were social withdrawal and attention problems; attention problems did not improve significantly. The abstract also flags loss of approximately one-third of participants by the four-year assessments. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)

These qualifications do not erase the IQ result, but they matter when deciding how confidently it should be generalized.

Earlier prenatal-choline studies do exist, but they assessed different populations, doses, ages and cognitive outcomes. A systematic review published in February 2025 necessarily predates this October 2026 result and cannot independently confirm it. Nor should findings about infant information processing or later attention be counted as replications of an eight-point preschool IQ difference. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11901549/)

The most faithful short version is:

> In one randomized trial published in October 2026, children whose mothers received a specific phosphatidylcholine regimen during pregnancy scored an average of 7.78 points higher on preschool IQ testing than the placebo group. IQ testing involved 103 children, the result was a secondary outcome, and independent replication remains unestablished. [pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896815/)
