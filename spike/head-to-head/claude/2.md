## Full answer (pasted verbatim)
**The claim traces to one estimate from one UC Riverside research group, popularized by the Washington Post in September 2024.** Unlike the goldfish stat, it isn't made up: it is a real calculation. But it has no independent confirmation, and its author has since revised it sharply downward.

## Where the wording changed

- **April 2023, paper:** GPT-3 "drinks" a 500 ml bottle for roughly 10–50 medium-length responses, depending on when and where it is deployed. The "medium-sized request" assumed up to about 800 words of input and 150–300 words of output. The model is GPT-3, and the unit is many responses per bottle.
- **April 2023, press:** Business Insider's headline says every 20 to 50 questions. The paper I read says 10–50, so the range may have changed between versions, but I couldn't check v1.
- **Sept. 2024, Post:** The unit flips from many responses per bottle to one 100-word email per bottle, and the model changes to GPT-4. TechRepublic reports about 519 ml, slightly more than a 16.9-ounce bottle. The Post framed it as roughly a bottle per email.
- **Late Sept. 2024, repeaters:** Living on Earth's intro gives the figure in "millimeters" and attributes the bottle claim to a Pew Research Center study. The Post cited Pew only for how many Americans have used ChatGPT, so the attribution is wrong.
- **2025–26, "per prompt":** The claim drifts from "per email" to "per prompt" or "per conversation." One explainer says the popular per-prompt version inflated the research's range by a factor of twenty to fifty. The Guardian's correction (item 4) is an example of this slide.
- **2026, walk-back:** About 15 ml per GPT-4 prompt, about 5 ml of it on-site.

## How many independent sources stand behind it?

**One source, and zero independent confirmations.**

- Every version comes from Ren's group. The Post said Ren calculated the water and electricity costs for GPT-4 at an average American data center. The Post therefore applied his method rather than verifying it.
- The 519 ml figure also counts upstream water at power plants. For the paper's own GPT-3 numbers, the U.S. average is about 2.2 ml on-site versus 14.7 ml off-site, out of 16.9 ml per request. The 519 ml also assumes a worst-case grid, and one secondary source puts the same email anywhere from 235 ml in Texas to 1,408 ml in Washington.
- Independent estimates run far lower, though they count different things. Google's median Gemini text prompt was 0.26 ml of water, counted under Google's own accounting. OpenAI's Sam Altman has cited about 0.32 ml, without publishing enough detail to reconcile it with the Post's estimate. A 2025 benchmarking study found under 2 ml for efficient models, though some reasoning models exceeded 150 ml per query.

I couldn't open the Post, the Atlantic, the Guardian or Masley's post directly, so the 2026 walk-back rests on secondary reports.
