## Full answer (pasted verbatim)
**The claim traces back to a website, not a study, and I found no independent measurement behind either number.** The 8-second human figure originates on a Statistic Brain web page, and the 9-second goldfish figure first appeared in an unsourced 2002 BBC line. Microsoft's 2015 report only reprinted them.

## Where the wording changed

- **2002:** The BBC line describes heavy web browsing producing a nine-second span. It is not a human average, and it asserts the goldfish equivalence without a source.
- **2012–13:** Statistic Brain turns this into "average attention span," with 12 seconds in 2000 and 8 seconds recently. The year label appears to shift from 2012 to 2013.
- **Feb 2014:** Koehn narrows it to "American."
- **Spring 2015:** Microsoft prints it as "average human attention span" in a report whose own research (about 2,000 Canadians surveyed, 112 with EEG) didn't produce those numbers. The report's foreword actually opens by asking whether digital is killing attention spans and answering "Think again."
- **May 2015:** Headlines recast it as a Microsoft finding about Canadians. The Telegraph, for example, says researchers surveyed 2,000 participants and found the average human attention span fell to eight seconds. The source line drops away.
- **Later versions:** Some sites say "8.25 seconds." I couldn't trace where the decimal comes from.

## How many independent sources stand behind it?

**Zero measurements, and effectively one origin per number.**

- **Human 8 seconds:** All roads lead to the Statistic Brain page. BBC found that the institutions it listed couldn't confirm any research, and Statistic Brain didn't respond. Time, the NYT, USA Today and the rest are copies of Microsoft's graphic, which is itself a copy of Statistic Brain.
- **Goldfish 9 seconds:** There is a separate lineage from the unsourced 2002 BBC line. BBC's later investigation found no evidence that fish have short attention spans.

One possible source for the 8–9 second numbers is a 2008 web-use study. Its median page-visit time was 9.4 seconds, and 52% of visits were under 10 seconds. That is a speculation by one blogger, not an established link, and the study measured page dwell time, not attention.

I read the BBC 2017 piece only through mirrors and excerpts, and the 2002 BBC page and the Wayback archives only through secondary descriptions. The Statistic Brain 2012–2015 details are therefore the weakest part of this trace.
