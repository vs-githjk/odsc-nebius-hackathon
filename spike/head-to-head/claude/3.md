## Full answer (pasted verbatim)
**The claim traces to two small 1967 lab experiments by Albert Mehrabian at UCLA.** The 7/38/55 formula is his own tentative combination of their results, and it concerns judgments of a speaker's *liking or attitude*, not communication in general. Its author has said that reading it as a rule for all communication is absurd, and I found no independent replication of it.

## The chain, newest to oldest

1. **Psychology Today, "The Body Language Myth," March 2020** (date from the URL). It credits the figures to the Mehrabian and Wiener study and describes it as using photos. It says 7% of judgment was based on words, 38% on tone and 55% on facial expressions. The photos belonged to the other study, so the attribution is blurred. URL: https://www.psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/surprise/202003/the-body-language-myth
2. **Lapakko, "Communication is 93% Nonverbal: An Urban Legend Proliferates," CTAMJ, Summer 2007.** He searched Google on Jan. 5, 2007 and examined 79 relevant sites; only four questioned the numbers. It also quotes Dr. Phil on CNN, May 26, 2002, saying 93 percent of communication is nonverbal.** URL: https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1000&context=ctamj
3. **Mehrabian's own page for *Silent Messages*** (© 1995–2016, undated). It gives Total Liking = 7% Verbal + 38% Vocal + 55% Facial and says the equations came from experiments on feelings and attitudes. It adds that they don't apply unless the speaker is talking about feelings or attitudes. URL: https://www.kaaj.com/psych/smorder.html
4. **Lapakko, "Three Cheers for Language," *Communication Education* 46(1), Jan. 1997.** It focuses on the Mehrabian and Ferris 1967 study and notes that applications of it overlook its limitations. It also reports Mehrabian's 1995 personal correspondence disavowing the generalization (see below).
5. **Burgoon (1985) and Hegstrom (1979).** By 1985, Burgoon was already calling the 93% figure a much-repeated popular estimate based on faulty analysis. I only saw these through Lapakko's citations.
6. **Mehrabian, *Silent Messages* (Wadsworth, 1971; 2nd ed. 1981),** with *Nonverbal Communication* (Aldine-Atherton, 1972). Mehrabian points to pages 75–80 for his discussion of inconsistent messages. URL: https://archive.org/details/silentmessagesim00mehr
7. **Mehrabian & Ferris, *Journal of Consulting Psychology* 31(3): 248–252, 1967** (doi 10.1037/h0024648). This is the earliest source I found for the numbers. It says the combined effect of verbal, vocal and facial channels is "a weighted sum" with coefficients .07, .38 and .55. The authors don't show how they combined the two experiments to get this formula.
8. **Mehrabian & Wiener, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 6(1): 109–114, 1967** (doi 10.1037/h0024532). I could only confirm the year, not the month.

## Where the wording changed

- **What was measured:** In 1967, judges inferred a speaker's *attitude* toward a listener. Study 1 used single words in different tones, and Study 2 used the word "maybe" with photographed facial expressions. It later became "liking," then "communication," "meaning," "message" and "effectiveness." Lapakko found 63 of 79 sites (80%) used the numbers as a statement about communication in general.
- **"Facial" became "body language":** The 55% came from photographs of faces, not bodies. Some sites widened it further to gestures, posture and appearance.
- **Added "93% nonverbal":** The 38% and 55% were summed into a headline figure that the original papers don't state.
- **Other distortions:** One site recast it as memory, claiming 7 percent of recall was verbal. A 2023 Psychology Today post describes a nonverbal-to-verbal ratio of about 9:1 in Mehrabian's study.
- **The author's own correction:** Mehrabian wrote that implying words are only 7% of all communication is absurd, and that his findings concern feelings and attitudes.

## How many independent sources stand behind it?

**One originator and zero independent confirmations.**

- Both experiments come from one researcher's lab. The numbers derive from two studies, and neither compared the verbal, vocal and facial channels simultaneously. The main study used 37 female psychology majors and the single word "maybe," which makes the role of words largely irrelevant by design.
- Later critiques found the method wanting. They cite the artificial posed stimuli and note that one 1987 study found nonverbal dominance disappears when the experiment's purpose is camouflaged. I read these through a secondary page, not the originals.
- Repetition is not corroboration. In Lapakko's sample, only 16 of 79 sites named Mehrabian, and 75 passed the numbers along without qualification.

I read the 1967 papers only through secondary descriptions and could not open them. Psychology Today dates come from URL slugs. I didn't find an earlier popular article than the 1971 book, and I couldn't confirm whether the "Total Liking" form first appeared there or in later editions.
